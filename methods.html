How it works

Every number on RINKSENSE comes from the event log and broadcast player-and-puck tracking. This page explains how each one is built, how accurate it is, and what it can't see.

Source

The data

Ten full games of Stathletes' public hockey dataset. Each game has an event log (shots, passes, recoveries, zone entries, faceoffs, penalties, with the skater counts at every event), a shift chart, and 30-frames-per-second x/y positions for players and the puck, estimated from the broadcast video. Coordinates are in feet with center ice at (0, 0).

Cleaning

Cleaning the tracking

  • Camera cuts. When the broadcast jumps ahead (stoppages, replays), the frame sequence skips. Every cut is marked, and nothing is ever smoothed or interpolated across one.
  • Game clock. Stray clock readings (a "20:00" mid-period, "0:00" before an OT faceoff) are replaced using a 5-second rolling median, and the clock is forced to only count down.
  • Puck. Points far from their neighbors are treated as misreads, gaps up to 1 second are filled inside the same camera shot, and the path is lightly smoothed.
  • Players off camera. The replay keeps every player the shift chart says is on the ice, drawing untracked ones faded at their last or next known spot. These estimated positions never feed any stat.
Model

Expected goals (xG)

The chance an unblocked shot becomes a goal, from a ridge-regularized logistic regression trained on every unblocked shot in the dataset. Inputs: distance and angle to the net (each team's direction comes from where it shot that period), rebound (a same-team attempt in the previous 3 seconds), one-timer, traffic, deflection, and power play.

Accuracy is measured leave-one-game-out: each game is scored by a model that never saw it. A team's xG is the sum over its unblocked shots; blocked shots get no xG.

Model

Win probability

Ten games are far too few to train a win model on outcomes, so RINKSENSE uses a transparent scoring model instead.

  • Each team's remaining goals follow a Poisson process. Its scoring rate starts at the league average and moves toward its own xG pace as the game goes, with the league average counting as one period (20 minutes) of evidence.
  • During a power play, the expected goals for its remaining time are scaled by the power-play and shorthanded multipliers measured across all ten games.
  • A tie after regulation goes to a 5-minute sudden-death overtime using the same rates, then a 50/50 shootout.

Turning points are the three moments (goals or penalties) where one team's win probability moved the most in a single second.

Early in a game the model leans on xG, so a team that is out-chancing its opponent can be favored at 0–0. Accuracy (Brier score: lower is better, a coin flip scores 0.25) is measured on these same ten games, so treat it as a sanity check rather than proof.

Tracking

Possession and time with puck

In each tracked frame, the nearest skater (goalies excluded) within 6 feet of the puck has it. Summed per player that's time with puck; summed per team it's possession. Frames where nobody is within 6 feet, or the puck isn't tracked, count for nobody.

Event log

Power plays

Each penalty opens a power-play window for the other team. It ends at the 2:00 expiry, at a power-play goal, or when the skater counts go back to even (offsetting minors). Coincidental minors, delayed-penalty extra attackers and late pulled goalies are never counted. The replay's countdown always runs to the normal expiry so it never hints at an early goal. The data doesn't mark double minors or majors, so every power play is treated as 2:00.

Tracking

Skating and movement

Broadcast tracking jitters, and the tracker sometimes swaps two players for a frame, which would invent impossible sprints. So each player's track is split wherever they would have moved more than 3 feet in 1/30 of a second (90 ft/s), positions are smoothed over half a second, and pieces shorter than 1 second are dropped.

  • Top speed: the 98th percentile of a player's speed within each minute of play, maximum across the game, so one bad frame can't set a record.
  • Burst acceleration: the same, for speed gained over 1 second.
  • Bursts: going from under 10 ft/s to over 20 ft/s within 2 seconds.
  • Speed zones: standing (under 3 ft/s), cruising (3 to 14), striding (14 to 22), sprinting (22 and up).
  • Feet per minute: distance per minute the player was tracked, split by whether their team or the opponent had the puck.
  • Shift-start speed: average speed in the first 10 seconds of each shift from the shift chart. A downward trend across the game is a fatigue signal.

The replay's Movement tab adds up whole minutes of play up to the current moment, so it never shows numbers from later in the game.

Tracking

Team shape

  • Spacing: average distance between tracked teammates, in frames with at least three of them on camera.
  • Offensive-zone time: tracked time the team has the puck past the blue line in its attacking zone.
  • Reaction burst: after a team loses the puck, the best 1-second speed gain by its tracked skaters in the next 2 seconds, averaged over turnovers.
Event log

Pass networks

Built from completed passes (a "Play" event with a receiver). Each player sits at the average location of the passes they made or received, mirrored so the team always attacks left to right. Line width is the number of passes between two players.

Players

Player pages

Each skater's numbers are added up across every game they played. A player is a team plus a jersey number (the data is anonymized), and the skater list comes from the shift charts, so goalies are left out.

  • Counting stats: goals, shots on goal and xG come from the event log; estimated assists (A*) use the same rule as the replay. Time on ice comes from the shift chart; time with puck from tracking.
  • Rates: xG per 60 is xG divided by time on ice, times 60, so it covers the whole game. The skating rates (feet per minute, sprint share, time with puck as a share of tracked time) use tracked minutes only, because a player off camera can't be measured. Top speed and burst acceleration are the best single-game values, counting only games with at least 2 tracked minutes.
  • Percentiles: a player's percentile is the share of other qualified skaters they beat (ties count half). To qualify, a skater needs at least 10 tracked minutes across all games. Players under that still get a rank against the qualified group, shown faded and labeled as rough.
  • Shot map: every attempt is mirrored so the player always shoots at the right-hand net, using the direction their team attacked that period. Dot size is xG; blocked shots have no xG. Each dot links to the replay a few seconds before the shot.
  • Chemistry: completed passes (a "Play" event with a receiver) made to or received from each teammate, across all games.

Most skaters only appear in one or two of the ten games, so percentiles compare small samples. Treat them as a description of these games, not a scouting grade.

Honesty

Limits

  • Camera coverage. Tracking only exists while a player is in the broadcast shot, so movement stats are rates per tracked minute, and totals undercount.
  • Partial periods. Two third periods only have tracking for their last few minutes. Events before that still count; tracking-based stats for those stretches are missing.
  • Small sample. Ten games. The xG model is validated on games it never saw; win probability is checked on the same games it describes.
  • Estimates. Assists (A*) are estimated from the last teammates to touch the puck, since the event log doesn't record them.
